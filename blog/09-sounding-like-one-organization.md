# A Nonprofit's Guide to Sounding Like One Organization, Not Five Writers

Nonprofits have a specific version of this problem, worth naming on its own. A typical small-to-medium nonprofit has program staff writing about services, a development team writing to donors, an executive director writing board updates, and maybe a part-time or volunteer comms person trying to hold social media and the newsletter together&mdash;often with no formal communications background among any of them. Every one of these people is writing on the organization's behalf. None of them were ever in the same room agreeing on how the organization should sound.

Here's what that produces, and what actually addresses it.

## The donor-facing consistency problem

Donors experience your organization primarily through what they read: thank-you letters, impact reports, campaign emails, the annual report. When program terminology shifts between documents, or the tone swings from warm to corporate and back, it doesn't read as a style quirk&mdash;it reads as a lack of attention to detail, which is exactly the impression a donor-funded organization can least afford to give.

## The program-naming problem

Nonprofits tend to accumulate named programs, initiatives, and campaigns faster than they standardize how those names are written. A program that's capitalized one way on the website, another way in a grant application, and a third way in a newsletter creates a small but real credibility gap&mdash;particularly with funders and grant reviewers who read closely and notice.

## The population-language problem

How you refer to the people you serve&mdash;members, clients, participants, families, individuals with lived experience&mdash;is one of the highest-stakes consistency questions a nonprofit faces, and it's also one of the fastest-evolving. Language that was standard five years ago may not be now. Without a written, periodically updated reference, this knowledge lives in whoever's been there longest, and leaves when they do.

## The volunteer and board-member problem

Nonprofits often have people writing on their behalf who aren't staff at all&mdash;board members drafting a fundraising appeal, volunteers running a chapter's social media. These are exactly the writers least likely to have absorbed "how we sound" through osmosis, because they're not in the office day to day. A written reference matters more for this group, not less.

## What ties all of this together

Every one of these is a version of the same underlying gap: no shared, written reference for voice, terminology, and formatting&mdash;the thing generally called a **style guide**, though nonprofits often reach for other words first (brand voice guidelines, communications guidelines, a style sheet). Whatever it's called, it does the same job: it takes decisions that were being made independently by every writer, every time, and makes them once, in writing, for everyone to use.

For a nonprofit specifically, this tends to pay off fastest in exactly the areas above&mdash;donor trust, program-name consistency, and getting new volunteers or board members writing on-brand faster than "trial and error over several email drafts."
