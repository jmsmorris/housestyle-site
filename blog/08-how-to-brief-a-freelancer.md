# How to Brief a Freelancer or New Hire on Your Organization's Voice

You've found a good freelancer, or hired someone promising, and now you need their first piece of writing to actually sound like your organization&mdash;not like a stranger's best guess at it. Most briefing conversations skip straight to the assignment ("we need a newsletter about the spring campaign") without ever covering the thing that determines whether the draft comes back usable: how you actually want it to sound.

Here's what an effective voice briefing covers, whether you're onboarding a new hire or handing off a single freelance project.

## Give them three to five words, not a vibe

"Make it sound like us" is not a briefing&mdash;it's a guess disguised as an instruction. Instead, give three to five concrete words: warm, direct, a little irreverent, never salesy. Concrete adjectives are something a writer can actually apply, in a way that "our brand voice" alone never quite is.

## Show them, don't just tell them

Pair the adjectives with two or three real examples of writing you consider on-voice, and ideally one example of something that missed the mark and why. Writers pattern-match faster from examples than from descriptions&mdash;a well-chosen sample piece of content does more work in five minutes than a paragraph of description does in twenty.

## Hand over your terminology, explicitly

This is the part most briefings skip entirely, and it's often the fastest way to make a draft look wrong even when the tone is right: incorrect program names, inconsistent capitalization, the wrong term for the people you serve. Before a new writer starts, they need the specific nouns your organization uses&mdash;not just the general tone.

## Tell them how tone shifts by situation

A donor thank-you and a crisis update shouldn't sound identical, and a writer who's only seen one type of your content won't know that unless you say so directly. A quick note&mdash;"routine updates are warm and brief; anything sensitive slows down and gets more formal"&mdash;prevents a lot of awkward first drafts.

## Give them a first-draft checklist, not just a debrief

Correcting mistakes after they happen teaches a writer slowly, over several rounds. A short checklist handed over *before* they start&mdash;capitalization rules, a few words to avoid, the Oxford comma question&mdash;lets them self-correct before you ever see the draft.

## The pattern underneath all of this

Every point above is really describing the same solution from different angles: a **style guide**. Not a formal, intimidating document&mdash;just a written answer to the questions above, built once, handed to every new writer from that point forward instead of explained fresh each time. The organizations that onboard freelancers and new hires fastest aren't the ones with the most talented briefers. They're the ones who wrote the briefing down once.
