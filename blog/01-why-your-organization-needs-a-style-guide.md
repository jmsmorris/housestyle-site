# Why Does My Organization Need a Style Guide?

If you've ever caught your organization calling itself a "Non-Profit" in one email and a "nonprofit" in the next—or referring to the same program by three different names across your website, newsletter, and social media—you've already found the reason you need a style guide.

It's a small thing. It's also the thing that quietly tells your audience whether they're dealing with an organization that has its act together.

## What a style guide actually is

A style guide isn't a grammar textbook, and it isn't the same thing as a brand guide (though the two work well together). A brand guide covers how your organization *looks*—logo, colours, fonts. A style guide covers how your organization *sounds*: word choice, tone, punctuation, formatting, and the specific terms unique to your work.

Think of it as the answer key to every "wait, how do we usually say this?" question your team has ever asked in a Slack thread.

## The problem it solves

Most organizations don't set out to sound inconsistent. It happens gradually, as a natural side effect of growth:

- More people are writing on the organization's behalf—staff, volunteers, board members, freelancers.
- Content spreads across more channels—website, email, social, donor reports, grant applications, event signage.
- Institutional knowledge lives in people's heads instead of on paper, so every new hire or volunteer has to learn "how we do things" by trial and error.

None of this is anyone's fault. It's just what happens when an organization gets busy without ever writing down its own rules.

## Why it matters more than it seems to

A style guide might feel like a "nice to have" compared to fundraising, programming, or member services. In practice, it touches all three:

**Trust.** Donors and members notice inconsistency, even if they couldn't tell you why something felt slightly off. A consistent voice reads as competence. An inconsistent one reads as disorganization—even when the organization behind it is anything but.

**Speed.** Every writer who has to guess at a style decision is a writer who's slower than they need to be. A style guide turns "how do we usually phrase this?" from a five-minute Slack detour into a ten-second lookup.

**Onboarding.** New staff, volunteers, and freelancers ramp up faster when there's a document that answers most of their early questions before they have to ask.

**Brand equity.** Every piece of content is a small deposit into how your organization is perceived. Consistency compounds that value. Inconsistency erodes it.

## Who feels this most

Nonprofits and sport organizations are especially prone to this gap, for a simple reason: both sectors tend to run lean on communications staff relative to how much content they produce. A national sport organization might have one communications coordinator writing for member clubs, media, athletes, and sponsors—often without a single shared reference for how any of it should sound. A growing nonprofit might have program staff, an executive director, and a part-time comms hire all writing donor-facing copy independently.

Neither is a failure of the team. It's a gap in infrastructure—and it's a genuinely easy one to close.

## A simple test

Pull up your last five pieces of published content—a newsletter, a social post, a program page, a donor letter, an email signature. Look specifically at:

- How you write your own organization's name and program names
- Whether numbers are spelled out or written as numerals
- Whether you use the Oxford comma
- How you refer to the people you serve

If the answers aren't consistent across all five, you've got a style guide gap—and it's costing you more polish than you probably realize.

Worth knowing: this is sometimes called a "brand voice guide" or "editorial guidelines" instead—see [how those terms differ](style-guide-brand-voice-glossary.html) if you're not sure which one you're looking for.
