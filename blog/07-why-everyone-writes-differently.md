# Why Does Everyone on My Team Write Differently?

You've probably noticed it without being able to name it: the newsletter sounds like one person, the social posts sound like another, and the last grant application sounded like neither of them. Nobody did anything wrong. This is just what happens when several people write on an organization's behalf without ever agreeing, out loud, on how that organization sounds.

Here's why it happens, and what actually fixes it.

## It's not a skill problem

The instinct is often to assume someone needs better writing training. Usually that's not it. Most people writing on your organization's behalf are perfectly good writers&mdash;they're just making dozens of small, reasonable decisions independently, with no shared reference to check against. One person writes "Non-Profit," another writes "nonprofit." One person's emails sound warm and casual; another's sound formal and careful. Neither is wrong. They just don't match.

## Every writer is filling gaps with their own judgment

When there's no written answer to "how do we usually say this," every writer answers the question themselves, differently, every time. Multiply that across a newsletter, a website, social media, donor letters, and grant applications, and the small inconsistencies add up into something a reader can feel, even if they can't articulate what's off.

## New people make it more visible, not less

A new hire, volunteer, or freelancer has no history to draw on, so their version of "how we sound" comes from guessing&mdash;reading old content, taking their best shot, and getting corrected after the fact. That's a slow way to onboard someone, and it's also the moment the inconsistency becomes most obvious, since a new writer's guesses rarely match everyone else's guesses either.

## The fix isn't more meetings

The instinct to solve this with more communication&mdash;more check-ins, more review cycles, more people copied on every email&mdash;usually just slows things down without actually fixing the underlying gap. The problem isn't a lack of communication about writing. It's a lack of a shared, written reference that answers the recurring questions once, instead of re-litigating them every time.

That reference has a name, even if you've never gone looking for it: a **style guide**. Not a grammar textbook, and not a brand deck&mdash;a working document that answers the specific questions your team keeps re-asking: how do we write our program names, what's our tone in a donor email versus a crisis update, do we use the Oxford comma, what do we call the people we serve.

## What changes once it exists

Nothing about your team's writing *talent* changes. What changes is that the guessing stops. New hires ramp up faster because there's something to hand them. Freelancers produce on-brand drafts sooner. And the small inconsistencies that quietly signal disorganization to donors and members start disappearing, one small decision at a time.
