# Your New Logo Is Out. Your Words Haven't Caught Up.

Your visuals might change. If your language stays the same, your rebrand isn't finished.

A new logo is easy to launch. It goes up, the announcement goes out, and everyone can see the difference on day one. Words are harder to see. They sit in email signatures, grant applications, partner credits, boilerplate, and old PDFs. A logo file can't update any of them.

## What two announcements covered

This spring, a provincial sport body announced a new name and a new visual identity. Its announcement says the new brand will appear gradually across events, communications, programming, and digital platforms over the coming months. It doesn't say what happens to the old name or the old materials.

A national sport body also unveiled a new logo this spring, as part of what it calls a refreshed identity. Its release covers the mark and the visual system. It doesn't address written voice or tone.

I can't see inside either organization, and a press release is not a plan. Both may have more underway than they announced. Still, as far as I can tell, neither announcement describes how the writing will change. The visuals get the detail.

## When the look changes and the voice doesn't

Sarah Durham of [Big Duck](https://bigduck.com), a communications firm that works only with nonprofits, [says rebrands used to mean a new logo and new colours](https://bloomerang.com/webinar/video-how-does-rebranding-impact-nonprofits). In the past five to 10 years, she says, more people have come to treat messaging as part of the job.

The words don't always have to change. Sometimes a refresh is only visual, and the language already fits. But when a new visual identity arrives as part of a refreshed brand package, writing that still matches the old brand voice can clash with it.

## What I'd settle before launch day

Big Duck says the name decision has to come first, because so much else depends on it. The writing works the same way. Before launch, I'd write down answers to four plain questions:

- How is the new name written on first mention, and after that?
- What do we call the old name while people still know us by it?
- Which documents get updated now, and which get a note?
- Where does someone searching for the old name end up?

A Canadian charity-consulting guide makes the last point on its own: if you rename, supporters searching for your old name should still be able to find you.
